WHY THIS MATTERS

Forest sustainability
cannot be measured by carbon alone.

Forests support climate, biodiversity, water, soil, timber and local livelihoods all at once. That is why no single policy or number can explain how a forest should be managed.
Forests need a system that combines funding, people and information and keeps management going over a long time horizon.

This page is a reader-friendly summary of the Notion research note “How Japanese forestry can become more sustainable?”.

01 / BACKGROUND

The forest resources exist.
The challenge is the foundation to keep managing them.

About two-thirds of Japan’s land is forest. The plantation forests established after the war have grown and could serve as a timber resource, but they need continuous care after planting, such as thinning and replanting.

As imported timber increased, domestic forestry became less profitable, and mountain villages saw their populations shrink and age. Where ownership boundaries and forest data have not been properly sorted out, even drawing up a management plan, carrying it out and checking the results becomes difficult.

02 / POLICY INSTRUMENTS

Use four instruments
in combination.

Market forces, voluntary standards, subsidies and the legal system. Each has its role and its limits.

01 / MARKET

J-Credit

Makes greenhouse-gas removals from forest management and planting visible as credits.

Strength
Attracts funding and can link corporate climate action with forest management.
Limit
Tends to focus narrowly on carbon; permanence, accounting and price must be explained.
02 / VOLUNTARY

Forest certification

Schemes such as FSC and PEFC, in which a range of stakeholders confirm that forests are managed responsibly.

Strength
Improves market access and management standards, and helps explain management to consumers.
Limit
Costs money to obtain and maintain, which can be a burden for small owners.
03 / PUBLIC SUPPORT

Thinning & replanting support

Encourages thinning and replanting, supporting forest growth and the forest’s role as a carbon sink.

Strength
Gets funding to the field quickly and makes fair participation easier to secure.
Limit
Forests take time to mature, and the problems of labour shortages and species diversity remain.
04 / REGULATION

Forest Act & planning system

Coordinates forest use through national and local government plans, notifications and permits.

Strength
Can align production and conservation with regional plans.
Limit
Depends on the knowledge, staffing and monitoring capacity of government, and has running costs too.

04 / ECONOMIC VALUE

Think about economic value through the many benefits forests create.

If value is reduced to the price of carbon, important benefits that sustain management disappear from view.

01

Timber & local industry

Produces timber, food, fuel and other resources, and supports jobs in harvesting, processing and distribution.

02

Climate & carbon

Verifies removals and long-term wood use, and links them to corporate decarbonisation investment.

03

Water, soil & disaster prevention

Supports the foundations of daily life: watershed protection, soil conservation and lower disaster risk.

04

Biodiversity & culture

Wildlife habitat, landscape, education and recreation hold value that is hard to measure by price alone.

What forest evaluation needs

Evaluation needs a record of forest boundaries, species, growth, carbon removals, water quality and management history, kept with GIS and similar tools, and a continuing check on whether funding reached field work and management outcomes. The important thing is not to force every value into money but to separate what can be measured from what must be explained.

05 / RECOMMENDATIONS

From now on, make full use of AI and every policy and tool, measure, and deliver to local communities.

  1. 01

    Combine the four policy instruments

    Use J-Credit, certification, support schemes and the Forest Act to fit each forest’s purpose and local conditions.

  2. 02

    Put non-carbon values into the plan

    Include biodiversity, water quality, soil, and social and economic aspects among the criteria for management plans and certification, and check them regularly.

  3. 03

    Pool small holdings to lower costs

    Through forest owners’ cooperatives, joint local management and group certification, share the measurement and certification costs that are too heavy for one owner alone.

  4. 04

    Connect private funding to field work

    Allocate funding to sorting out ownership, forest maintenance, safety, training and data, so that long-term local jobs and management capacity remain.
